Is a Home Battery Worth It? Honest Payback Math (2026)

📅 Sep 7, 2026 · Lucas Ballek · 11 min read · every number below comes from this site's deterministic simulator, not guesswork

The short answer: a home battery is worth it where grid power costs roughly $0.25–0.45/kWh or where evening peak prices run about 1.5–3x the off-peak rate — there a 2–6 kWh LFP or sodium-ion bank typically repays its component cost in about 1–4 years. Where power is cheap (about $0.10–0.15/kWh), flat-rate, and reliable, the same battery stretches past 5–10 years and the panels alone do most of the work.

What a home battery actually costs in 2026

Battery prices are always quoted per usable kWh — what you can actually cycle night after night — not nameplate. A lead-acid bank only gives you about 50% of its sticker, while LFP and sodium-ion give you about 80–90%. The honest 2026 spread is:

  • LFP (LiFePO4), usable: roughly $80–125/kWh self-built, landed DIY vs $110–165/kWh budget retail pack.
  • Sodium-ion, usable: roughly $88–138/kWh landed DIY vs $110–175/kWh retail — a 10–15% premium while cell lines ramp.
  • Lead-acid AGM, usable: roughly $240–330/kWh landed DIY vs $280–380/kWh retail — per usable kWh, because only half the nameplate counts.
  • Rest of system: panels about $0.16–0.38/W, hybrid inverter about $85–260/kW, plus install labor about $12–30 per usable kWh.

Worked example: 10 kWh/day in Honolulu ($0.40–0.44/kWh)

A household using 10 kWh/day at $0.40–0.44/kWh spends roughly $1,460–1,610/year. Simulated against five years of hourly satellite weather:

The battery's share of the 60% row is small: 2 kWh at $80–125/kWh DIY is $160–250. Payback here is simple division, and the true break-even the tool reports is only slightly later, because an LFP bank at this duty needs 0–1 swaps in 20 years.

Same house in Germany ($0.35–0.45/kWh, hard winters)

At $0.35–0.45/kWh a 10 kWh/day German household spends roughly $1,280–1,640/year — similar pain to Honolulu, but December sun is a fraction of June's. A 60–80% cut stays cheap (about 2.5–3.5 kW of panel with 2–4 kWh of storage, roughly $600–2,200 by scope), while chasing the last 15–20% through winter demands arrays sized on the darkest week of five years of weather. Build toward 60–80% now, expand later.

Sodium-ion's cold tolerance (charges down to about −20 °C vs LFP's 0 °C floor without a heated enclosure) is genuinely useful in unheated outbuildings — at a 10–15% premium nearly invisible over 20 years.

Time-of-use tariffs: the battery's second paycheck

Where the utility charges cheap off-peak nights ($0.10–0.20/kWh) against expensive evening peaks ($0.35–0.55/kWh), the battery earns even without extra panels: kWh shifted/day × (peak − off-peak) × 0.90–0.92 × 330–365 days. Shifting 2 kWh/day across a $0.25–0.35 spread is worth roughly $165–235/year; across a thin $0.10–0.15 spread only $65–100/year. Same battery, 2–3x different income.

When a battery does NOT pay

  • Cheap flat-rate power ($0.10–0.15/kWh). A 2 kWh bank saves $70–110/year — a 2–5 year story on paper stretching past 5–10 years once inverter, wiring, and labor count.
  • Lead-acid at daily-cycling duty. The bank dies every 1–2 years; 8 counted swaps over 20 years turn a cheap bank into the most expensive row.
  • Oversizing for the last few percent. Going from 80% to 95% often needs 50–100% more storage for 15 points of cut. Stop at the knee of the curve.
  • Backup-only duty with a reliable grid. A bank that sits full 350 days a year earns nothing; call it insurance, not payback.
  • Export-paid households. Where feed-in credits pay $0.08–0.20/kWh, exporting midday solar can beat storing it.

How much battery is enough?

For bill-cutting, cover your evening peak window (4–9 pm), not your whole day. For a 10 kWh/day household that is typically 2–4 kWh usable: 2 kWh moves the cut from roughly 40% to 55–65%; 4 kWh reaches 70–80%; beyond 6 kWh each added kWh buys visibly less.

Frequently asked

Is a home battery worth it without solar panels?

Sometimes, under time-of-use rates: a grid-charged bank shifting 2–4 kWh/day across a $0.25–0.45 spread earns roughly $165–400/year and can repay a $160–500 DIY bank in 1–3 years. Under a flat tariff it rarely pays — the panels create the cheap energy to shift.

Is it cheaper to build the battery myself?

Usually yes at 5 kWh and above: self-built LFP lands around $80–125 per usable kWh shipped and duty-paid, versus roughly $110–165 for budget retail packs.

LFP or sodium-ion for home storage?

LFP is the mature default with the lowest lifetime cost. Sodium-ion costs 10–15% more and buys cold-weather charging to about −20 °C — pick it for unheated sheds and cold climates, LFP everywhere else.

How long does a home battery last?

At daily cycling: LFP 14–20 years, sodium-ion 13–18 years, lead-acid AGM 1–2 years. Gentle time-of-use-only duty stretches each band 20–50%.
