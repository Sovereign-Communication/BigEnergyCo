Hawaii's 2026 Hurricanes: Backup Power Lessons

📅 Oct 12, 2026 · Lucas Ballek · 8 min read

The short answer: in 2026, Hawaii learned that the grid is a single point of failure — and that a modest battery system is the cheapest insurance against it. Three storms, one lesson: size your critical loads, keep a battery between you and the next outage, and let the HECO incentive help pay for it.

The season, by the numbers

  • Lala — landfall on the Big Island Aug 15: first hurricane-force gusts in 155 years, federal disaster declaration.
  • Lowell — 92% of Kaua'i lost power.
  • Nolo — early October, no land impacts. The scare that made everyone finally check their batteries.

First direct-hit season since Iniki (1992). Thirty-four years of luck ran out in one summer.

What an outage actually costs

  • Medical devices — CPAPs, oxygen concentrators, refrigerated medication. These don't negotiate with the utility.
  • Lost work-from-home income — no power, no wifi, no paycheck that day.
  • Water — on catchment or a well, no power means no pump. Toilets, showers, everything.
  • Communications — cell towers carry hours of backup, not days. When the towers go dark, so does the emergency information.

The backup-power math (honest version)

Critical loads — fridge, lights, phones, wifi, medical — run 2–4 kWh/day for most homes. That is a small system: 1.5–2 kW of panels with about 5 kWh of battery rides through multi-day outages with sun between storms.

Generator vs battery, honestly: a generator is cheaper up front and runs as long as you have fuel — which is exactly what runs out after a storm, because stations need power to pump. A battery is silent, fuel-free, instant-on, and recharges every sunrise. For the critical-load band, the battery wins on every axis except sticker price.

The money hook: HECO pays you to stop depending on them

Hawaii's BYOD Plus incentive pays $400/kW of enrolled battery capacity, plus another $400/kW LMI adder for low-to-moderate-income households. A 5 kW battery enrolled is $2,000 toward the system — $4,000 if you qualify for the adder. The utility is paying you to need it less.

What to do this week

  1. List your critical loads — fridge, medical, comms, water pump. Write down the watts.
  2. Size it free — run your numbers through the calculator against five years of real weather data.
  3. Check the BYOD incentive — confirm current rates and your eligibility with HECO before you buy.
  4. Build or buy before next season — batteries ordered in winter are installed by summer.

Frequently asked

What size battery backup do I need for a hurricane outage in Hawaii?

Critical loads — fridge, lights, phones, wifi, medical — run 2–4 kWh/day for most homes. About 1.5–2 kW of panels with 5 kWh of battery covers multi-day outages when the sun returns between storms.

Does HECO really pay you for installing a battery?

The BYOD Plus incentive pays $400 per kW of enrolled battery capacity, plus a $400/kW adder for low-to-moderate-income households. Confirm current rates and eligibility with HECO — incentives change.

Generator or battery for hurricane backup?

A generator is cheaper up front but needs fuel, which is scarce after a storm. A battery is silent, instant-on, fuel-free, and recharges from the sun. For critical loads, batteries win on everything except sticker price.

Will my solar panels work when the grid goes down?

Only with a hybrid inverter plus battery and proper islanding hardware. Standard grid-tied panels shut down during outages for anti-islanding safety.
